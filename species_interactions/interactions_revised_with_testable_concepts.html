HOW: Competition can occur through different mechanisms


  • Exploitative competition
    • organisms indirectly reduce the availability of a shared limiting resource
    • food, nutrients, light, water, space


  • Interference competition
    • organisms directly prevent others from accessing a resource
    • fighting, territoriality, chemical inhibition


  • Both mechanisms can occur within or between species

Gause’s Law: Theory of Competitive Exclusion


Two species cannot stably coexist if they occupy the same niche and use the same limiting resources


  • A competitive winner and loser should emerge


  • The weaker competitor may undergo local extinction or shift resource use / niche space

Competitive exclusion is hard to detect in nature


  • Resources are variable and often limiting
    • both competitors may struggle


  • Species usually compete for multiple resources
    • exclusion based on a single resource can be too simple


  • Complete niche overlap is rare outside simplified experiments


  • Similar distributional patterns can arise from several ecological mechanisms


  • TAKE HOME: Greater niche overlap = greater potential competition

How species coexist despite competition


  • Resource partitioning / niche differentiation
    • species reduce niche overlap by using resources differently
    • differences can occur in space, time, or resource form


  • Character displacement
    • natural selection favors greater trait differences where competing species coexist


  • Less niche overlap → weaker competition → easier coexistence
    • lions, cheetahs, and wild dog populations all persist

Biotic interactions can restrict—or expand—realized niches

  • Competition (− / −)
    • reduces access to limiting resources
    • can exclude a species from a suitable habitat
    • can shrink the realized niche
  • Mutualism (+ / +)
    • provides resources that improve fitness
    • allows persistence under difficult conditions
    • can expand where a species is able to persist through facilitation

Mutualism: benefits are not free

  • Both partners receive a fitness benefit—but both usually incur a cost


  • Flower ↔ pollinator
    • food ↔ pollen transfer


  • Plant ↔ mycorrhizal fungus
    • carbon ↔ nutrients / water


  • Fruit-producing plant ↔ animal
    • food ↔ seed dispersal


For any mutualism: What does each partner gain, and what does each partner pay?

Mutualisms are context dependent


  • Net effect = benefits received − costs paid


  • Environmental conditions can change both sides of that relationship
    • resource availability
    • stress
    • abundance or identity of partners


  • A strongly beneficial interaction under one set of conditions may become weak, neutral, or even costly under another


  • TAKE HOME: “Mutualism” describes the net ecological outcome—not a permanently fixed relationship
